You're doing fine. That's what makes it strange.

The job pays. The work is competent. Nobody's worried about you. And somewhere underneath the calendar there's a question you don't say out loud, usually around Sunday evening: is this it?

Not despair. Just flatness. A sense that you're executing something rather than building it.

So you go looking. You read the books, you do the exercise with the four circles, you write "what would I do if money were no object" at the top of a page and stare at it. And nothing arrives.

Here's what the research actually says about that.

---

## Not having one is the normal condition

Stanford's William Damon has spent decades interviewing people about their sense of direction. He sorts them into four groups: the disengaged, the dreamers (aspirations, no plan), the dabblers (lots of activity, no commitment), and the purposeful.

Roughly one in five young people, and about a third of young adults, land in that last group.

Read that again, because it reframes the whole problem. Four in five people do not have a clear purpose they're actively pursuing. You are not behind. You are in the majority, and the feeling of being uniquely adrift is an artefact of everyone else's LinkedIn.

There's a second distinction that does more work than it looks like. Michael Steger's research separates the **presence** of meaning from the **search** for it — and found the two correlate only weakly. Searching is not the same as lacking. Someone actively looking and someone who quietly gave up are in materially different positions, even though both would tell you they haven't found it.

If you're reading this, you're searching. That's a state, not a deficit.

## The ikigai diagram isn't Japanese

You've seen it: four overlapping circles — what you love, what you're good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for — with *ikigai* at the intersection. It's on a thousand LinkedIn carousels and in most corporate away-days.

It was drawn by a British blogger.

The actual sequence: in 2011, Spanish author Andrés Zuzunaga published a four-circle diagram labelled *Propósito* — Spanish for purpose. Nothing to do with Japan. In 2014, Marc Winn watched a TED talk by Blue Zones researcher Dan Buettner that mentioned ikigai in passing, put Zuzunaga's diagram in a blog post, and swapped the centre label to "ikigai." [Winn has written about it himself](https://theviewinside.me/ikigai) — he's said, more or less, that he changed one word on someone else's diagram and it became a meme. It has since been seen by tens of millions of people and reproduced in the bestselling book on the subject.

Why it matters isn't pedantry. It's that the diagram makes a specific claim: your purpose sits at the intersection of all four circles, and if you're missing one, you're missing your life's potential. That is a demanding, career-shaped, all-or-nothing test — and it has no research behind it, because it was a career-planning graphic that acquired a Japanese word by accident.

The concept Japanese researchers actually study is smaller and much nearer. Mieko Kamiya's 1966 book is the foundational text, and neuroscientist Ken Mogi's framing — start small, release yourself, the joy of little things, being here now — locates ikigai in a morning coffee or a conversation as readily as in a vocation. No intersection required. Nothing to be paid for.

One honest note: a systematic review of the ikigai literature found definitions and measures have been used inconsistently across studies, so treat any single framing — including that one — as a lens rather than a finding.

## "Find your passion" has evidence against it

This one is a real experimental result, not a vibe.

O'Keefe, Dweck and Walton ran [five studies published in *Psychological Science* in 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797618780643) on what they call implicit theories of interest — whether you believe interests are fixed things waiting to be discovered, or things that get developed.

People holding the fixed view were less curious about anything outside their existing interests. They expected a found passion to come with limitless motivation. And when a new interest got difficult, their interest dropped away significantly faster than it did for people primed to think interests are built.

That last finding is the important one. If you believe purpose is something you *find*, then difficulty becomes evidence you found the wrong thing. Every hard patch reads as a signal to go back to looking. Which is an excellent way to spend a decade sampling.

The caveats, because they matter: these were mostly lab studies with student samples, the effects were modest, and mindset research as a field has had a bruising few years on replication. This is a well-designed finding worth acting on, not a law.

## What purpose does predict

The health literature here is genuinely large.

A [2016 meta-analysis in *Psychosomatic Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26630073/) pooled ten prospective studies covering 136,265 people and found a higher sense of purpose associated with lower all-cause mortality and fewer cardiovascular events. A [much larger 2026 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42417009/) combined published studies with individual participant data — 488,765 people, followed for up to 32 years — and found the same direction.

Now the part most articles skip. All of it is observational. Nobody has run a trial where they assign purpose to one group and withhold it from another, because you can't. And the reverse explanation is entirely plausible: people who are becoming unwell, or depressed, or exhausted report less sense of purpose *because* of that, not the other way round.

So: purpose travels with living longer. Whether it causes it is unproven. Anyone telling you a purpose statement will add years to your life is reading the abstract and ignoring the discussion.

## Purpose is downstream of action

Every credible thread in this literature points the same way: purpose is not excavated by introspection. It accumulates from things you actually did.

Damon's applied finding is that durable purpose grows out of activities people genuinely enjoy — not obligation, not service performed grimly — that also turn out to matter to someone other than themselves. Both halves, or it doesn't hold.

Viktor Frankl, writing from considerably worse circumstances than a flat Sunday, identified three routes to meaning: work or creation, love and relationship, and the stance you take toward suffering you can't remove. The useful part for most people is that they're alternatives. When the first one destabilises — a restructure, an industry shifting under you, a career that stops making sense — the other two are still there and are not consolation prizes.

And self-determination theory, the strongest evidence base in motivation research, gives you a diagnostic rather than a pep talk. A goal that feels dead is usually missing one of three things: **autonomy** (any real choice in how it's done), **mastery** (visible progress at getting better), or **purpose** (a connection to something beyond you). Name which one is absent and you have a problem you can act on, instead of a mood.

## What to do this week

Not a worksheet. Three small things, none of which can be failed.

**Collect data instead of answers.** For seven days, note two things at the end of each day: a moment you lost track of time, and a moment something you did mattered to someone else. Don't interpret. At the end of the week you'll have fourteen data points, which is more than you had, and considerably more than the four circles gave you.

**Run the diagnostic on one dead thing.** Take the part of your work that feels most pointless. Which is missing — autonomy, mastery, or purpose? Usually it's obvious within thirty seconds, and usually only one of them is actually fixable this month. Fix that one.

**Do the two-minute version.** Whatever you keep saying you'd like to try — write the first paragraph, send the one email, book the single class. Not to find out whether it's The Thing. Interest is built by contact, and you cannot develop an interest you have never touched.

---

## When it's something else

Flatness that covers everything, including things you used to enjoy, and that's lasted more than a couple of weeks, is worth taking to a GP. Anhedonia is a symptom, not a philosophical position, and it responds to treatment. This article is general information, not medical advice.
